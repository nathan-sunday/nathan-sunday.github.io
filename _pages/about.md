Welcome to my homepage!

I am a PhD candidate in the [Department of Economics](https://lsa.umich.edu/econ) at the University of Michigan and am on the **2026–2027 economics job market**. 

My research focuses on public finance with applications in developing countries. I examine how taxes, transfers, and institutional reforms shape firms, labor markets, and broader economic development. My current work uses administrative firm-level tax data from Uganda to study how property taxation and decentralization affect firm activity and the fiscal capacity of the central government, as well as their broader welfare implications. 

I previously worked as a Research Analyst at the [Economic Policy Research Centre (EPRC)](https://eprcug.org/), a policy research think tank in Uganda. In the summer of 2024, I also served as a PhD Fellow at the [National Planning Authority (Uganda)](https://npa.go.ug/), contributing to the preparation of the country's Fourth National Development Plan. I hold a Master of Arts in Economics and a Bachelor of Science in Education (Economics and Mathematics) from Makerere University.

<summary><strong>Abstract</strong> (click to expand)</summary>

Property taxation is widely viewed as a promising source of revenue for developing countries, yet evidence on its economic effects remains limited. Using administrative data from corporate and employee tax returns in a staggered difference-in-differences design, this paper shows that a property tax increase in Kampala, Uganda significantly reduced rates of firm survival. Business rental expenses increased by 6.3 percent, and firms responded by reducing formal employment and wages, with wage informalization accounting for most of the decline in the formal wage bill. Higher-earning employees of ongoing firms bore disproportionate shares of the wage declines. Within government, there were vertical fiscal externalities: the tax increase reduced central government income tax revenues by 30 percent of the amount it contributed to local property tax collections.  The estimates imply that property taxes have 53 percent marginal deadweight losses.
